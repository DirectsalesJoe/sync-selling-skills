# The value test

Read this first, every run. Every other step in the skill is judged against it, and when two rules seem to pull in different directions, this one wins.

An account-based campaign lives or dies on one thing: what you hand the person. The list, the research and the writing exist to deliver it. A plain message carrying something genuinely useful to the right person at the right moment gets replies. A beautifully written message carrying nothing gets ignored, and no rewrite saves it.

Joe's rule from the Sync Selling sessions: "always assume that unless you're giving something of value, the prospect isn't going to want to come and set time aside." And: "the thought of a 30 or 40 minute meeting is just too much in a cold outreach message."

## The bar

The bar is a message so useful they'd be happy to pay to receive it.

Ask it literally. If this thing were for sale, what would this person pay for it, and would they pay out of their own budget without asking anyone? Write the honest answer down in pounds. If the honest answer is nothing, it's a pitch with a gift's wrapping on, and it goes back to the workshop.

The idea has a name, the permissionless value proposition, or PVP. The term is Jordan Crawford's (Blueprint). Permissionless means you hand the value over before they've agreed to anything: no call, no sign-up, no reply needed to get it.

## Three ways to open, and which one to aim for

1. **Ask for the meeting.** Most cold messages do this. It asks a stranger for thirty minutes before they've had a reason to give one.
2. **Offer something.** "I've put together X for you. Would you have any objections if I send it over?" The thing already exists, made for them. They say yes in one word.
3. **Give it in the message.** The useful thing is right there in the email: the number, the list, the warning, the rewrite. They can act on it without replying.

Aim for the third. Use the second when the gift is too big or too personal for an email, a video, a document, a sheet with forty rows. Never use the first on a first touch.

When you offer, the thing has to exist before the email sends, and you say it in the past tense: "I've put together", never "I could put together". The past tense makes them curious about something real. The conditional asks them to imagine work you haven't done, and a promise from a stranger is worth nothing.

Joe's line for the ask: "Would you have any objections if I send it over?" Because "if someone replies yes, then we know that that person is potentially interested in the problems that we solve."

And his rule on the balance: "you want to make the value of the lead magnet very big, but the ask very, very small."

## The eight tests

Every candidate gift and every finished email is run through these, in this order. A gift has to pass all of the first five to be used. Tests six to eight are about how it's handed over, and a fail there means rewriting the email, while a fail on one to five means finding a better gift.

1. **Would they pay to receive it?** The bar above. Write the price they'd pay and why. A price below what an hour of their time is worth is a fail, because they'll spend that long deciding whether to trust you.
2. **Is it built on something real?** Your own data, your expertise, public records, or their own material reworked. A gift built on a guess is a guess. If you can't name the source, it goes.
3. **Is it tied to what you sell?** A reply should tell you they have the problem you solve. A useful gift about something you don't sell gets you a thank you and nothing else.
4. **Is it worth having if they never buy?** If the value only appears once they become a customer, it's a pitch. Check by imagining they read it, act on it and never speak to you. Are they better off? Then it passes.
5. **Is it specific to them?** A number, a name or a date about their business, and one decision they could make from it today. If the same message could go to their competitor with only the company name changed, it isn't specific enough for account-based outreach.
6. **The forward test.** Could they forward it to a colleague in the same job, who could act on it without any other context? If it needs a call to make sense, it's a pitch.
7. **Who is the sentence about?** Read every sentence and ask who it's about. Most should be about them, their situation or the thing you've handed them. A message where most sentences are about you is about your wallet, and they feel it in half a second.
8. **Is the ask small?** One line they can answer in a word. No calendar link on the first touch, no thirty minutes, no "let me know a good time".

## Where value comes from

Valuable gifts come from knowing something about their situation that they don't. That gap is the asset, and it comes from four places.

- **Your own data.** What you see across all your customers, anonymised: what the ones who got through this moment did, what the going rate is, where the jobs that go wrong go wrong. One customer sees their own business. You see dozens.
- **Your expertise.** What you see every week that they see once in a career. The fourth time you've watched a business go through a merger, you know which three things go wrong in month two.
- **Public records joined up.** Planning portals, company filings, licences, tenders, review sites, job ads, permit data. Every piece is public. Nobody has put the pieces together for their business, and doing it would take them a day they haven't got.
- **Their own material, reworked.** Their quote, their website, their reviews, their job ad, their newsletter, their proposal. You show them a better version of something they made. They can see the difference immediately, because it's theirs.

Two questions get you there.

**The break-in question.** If your best prospect broke into your office and could take one thing, one file, one spreadsheet, one answer from your head, what would they take? Push past the first answer. The second and third are usually better, because the first is what you'd like them to want.

**The full-day question.** If this person had a full day and unlimited access to everything you know, what would they ask you? You're answering that question for them before they ask it.

## Make it specific, then point it forward

A gift that lands carries a number, a name or a date, and one decision they can make from it. "Practices like yours" is vague. "The four practices above you on the map all answer their Google reviews, and you have 38 unanswered" is something they can check and act on today. (That practice is made up, from a Sync Selling clinic.)

Then point it forward. A benchmark on its own tells them where they stand. The gift tells them what happens next if they carry on, and what the ones who did well did instead.

And say what you don't know. "I don't know your numbers, so this might not apply" stops the message overclaiming, and it makes them run the sum against their own business, which is exactly what you want them doing.

## When the gift is a warning

Sometimes the most valuable thing you can hand someone is a risk they haven't seen. Cold, from a stranger, a warning can read as a threat, and people defend against threats.

The fix is to frame it as something others got caught by. "Three firms near you had their certificates lapse at the renewal date last year, and two lost a contract over it" lands better than "your certificate is about to lapse". Same fact, and the reader is the one who gets to avoid the mistake.

Once someone has told you the problem themselves, on a call or in a reply, you can say plainly what staying as they are costs. That rule is for later, and this one is for strangers.

## What kills the value

Each of these turns a gift back into a pitch. The checker and the reader both look for them.

- **The wallet message.** Every line is about what you do, dressed up as interest in them.
- **The signal as the opener.** "I saw you're hiring" or "congratulations on the new role" tells them you noticed, and that's all. The moment is your reason to write; their situation is what you write about.
- **Data theatre.** Every fact you found, laid out to prove you did your homework. They care what to do with it, nothing more.
- **The pitch dressed as a gift.** A gift they need a call to get. "I'd love to walk you through what we found."
- **The pain message passed off as a gift.** A message built from their likely problems that ends at your product. Ask who the value belongs to: if it only appears once they buy, it's a pitch.
- **The clone message.** The sentence they've had eight hundred times this quarter. It might be a good offer, but it reads like all the others.
- **Surveillance.** Personal details that are true and irrelevant. Knowing what they posted on holiday tells them you've been watching, and it gives them nothing they can use.
- **The made-up number.** A figure with no source, or a rounded one. One wrong number and they stop trusting every other line.

## The scorecard

Use this table in the value workshop for every candidate gift, and again in the value decision for each account. A gift is used only when the first five columns are all yes.

| Gift | They'd pay (£, and why) | Built on (source) | Tied to what we sell | Worth it if they never buy | Specific (number, name, date, decision) | Forward test | Cost to make per account | How many accounts it works for |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

The last two columns decide between gifts that both pass. A gift that takes a day per account suits ten accounts. A gift that takes ten minutes per account, once the template is built, suits two hundred. Pick the one that matches how many accounts are at the right moment: the interview's rough figure to start with, then the sample's count once you have it.

## The one test that beats the rest

Before the first email goes, show it to one real customer in the segment, or to someone who's sat in the buyer's chair. Tell them you didn't write it, so they can be honest. Ask whether they'd want it. If they say it would be really valuable to them, you're done. If they hesitate, believe the hesitation over every test above.
