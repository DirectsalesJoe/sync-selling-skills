# Step 1: the interview

The interview gives the rest of the run everything it can't find on the internet: what the user sells, who buys it, what they know that nobody else does, and what they're allowed to claim. Ask one question at a time, wait for the answer, and follow up when an answer is vague. A vague answer here becomes a vague email at the end.

The user starts it with something like the prompt from the Sync Selling session:

> We sell [what] into [who]. We're creating an ABM campaign. Interview me to work out who the perfect person to contact is, what the perfect time is, and what we could send or offer them that they'd find so useful they'd be happy to pay to receive it. It can come from our own data, our knowledge, our expertise, or data that's publicly available on the internet.

Before asking anything, read whatever the user has already given you or pointed you to: their website, case studies, a sales deck, call recordings, an existing customer list. Don't ask a question the material already answers. Ask them to confirm it instead.

Most of the interview's time goes on part 4, what they know that their buyers don't, because that's where the gift comes from.

## 1. What you sell, and to whom

- What do you sell, in one sentence, the way a customer would describe it to a friend?
- Who are your three best customers, and what did each one buy?
- What was going on in each of their businesses just before they bought? Push here. "They needed help" isn't an answer. "Their sales manager had just left and the owner was taking the calls" is.
- Who did you talk to and lose, and why?

The answer to the third question is the start of the trigger. Businesses rarely buy because of who they are. They buy because of something that just happened to them.

## 2. The person

- Inside the business, who feels this problem first, in their own week? Which job title?
- Who can say yes to buying from you without asking anyone else? Is it the same person?
- If it's two people, who would you rather open with, and why?
- What words do they use for the problem? (Get the actual phrases. "We keep losing quotes to cheaper firms" is worth more than "price sensitivity".)
- What have they tried already, and why didn't it work?

## 3. The moment

- What has to happen in a business for this problem to become urgent? A new hire, a hire leaving, a merger, a new site, a licence, a contract won or lost, a new season, a regulation date?
- Can that moment be seen from outside? Where would it show up: a job ad, a company filing, a planning portal, a press release, a register, a review site, LinkedIn?
- How long after the moment does the window stay open? A week, a month, a quarter?
- How many businesses do you think hit this moment each month, roughly?

Offer the ones that work for nearly everyone if they're stuck: job changes, a new senior hire, mergers, acquisitions, licences and permits. Then the rest, in `triggers-and-research.md`.

## 4. What you know that they don't

This part matters most. Spend the time here.

- If your best prospect broke into your office and could take one thing, one file, one spreadsheet or one answer from your head, what would they take? Ask for three answers, and push past the first.
- What do you see across all your customers that one customer, on their own, can't see?
- What do you know now that you wish every customer had known before they started with you?
- What's the most common expensive mistake you see businesses make at this moment?
- What do your customers ask you most often, in their first month?
- What data do you hold that could be anonymised and counted (prices, timings, results, what worked)?
- If you had a full day to research one of these businesses, what would you look for, and what would you send them?

Write the answers down word for word. They become the raw material for the value workshop.

## 5. What you can claim

The email can only claim what's written here. Nothing about the user gets invented, and no number either.

- What results have you got for customers, with the actual number, the timeframe and the kind of business? (If they say "around 30%", ask for the exact figure or leave it out.)
- Which customers can be named, and which have to stay anonymous ("a £100m technology reseller")?
- What experience do you have personally that a buyer in this segment would care about?
- What can't you claim? (A sector you haven't worked in, a size of business you haven't served.) This matters as much as what you can, because the email has to be honest when the buyer's need is outside it.

This becomes `proof-bank.md`.

## 6. The fences

- Who must never be contacted? Existing customers, anyone in a live deal, competitors, partners, anyone who's asked not to be contacted.
- Which countries and regions are in?
- Is there a size of business that's too big or too small to be worth it?

## 7. Volume and channel

- How many conversations a week can you handle if this works?
- Do you have a sending tool and warmed-up mailboxes already, or will this go from your own inbox?
- Do you have an email-finding tool, and which one? (Any will do. The skill works with whatever they have, or with none for a small campaign.)
- How do you sign your emails? Your full name, company, phone number and LinkedIn, and the link to your privacy notice for the opt-out line. If there's no privacy notice yet, say that one is needed before the first send.

Write two files into the campaign folder and show the user both before moving on.

`campaign-brief.md`, in this shape:


`proof-bank.md`, in this shape:

```
# Proof bank: what we're allowed to claim

Every claim an email makes about us must be a line in this file, quoted exactly.

| # | The claim, exactly as it can be said | The number and its source | Can the customer be named? |

## What we can't claim
```

Ask the user to correct both files. Then go to the value workshop.
