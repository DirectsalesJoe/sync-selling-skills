# The kinds of gift

The value workshop generates candidates across all eight kinds below before it settles on one, because the first idea is rarely the best and most people stop at it. Each kind says what it is, what you need to make it, when it fits, what it looks like in a few different trades, and the trap that turns it back into a pitch.

The trade examples are illustrations made up for this file. Every real gift is built from real sources, with nothing invented.

---

## 1. The benchmark

**How they compare with businesses like theirs, and what the ones doing well do differently.**

You need data across many businesses: your own customers, anonymised, or a public source you've counted properly. One customer's figures are never shown, only the pattern across them.

It fits when you've worked with enough businesses in their segment that a pattern is real, and when the comparison points at something they can change.

- A bookkeeping firm writing to restaurant groups: the three costs that rose fastest this year for groups their size, counted from the books it keeps, and which of those costs the groups that held their margin renegotiated first.
- A recruiter writing to engineering firms: how long it's taking to fill this exact role in this region this quarter, and the salary band the roles that filled fastest were advertised at.
- A software firm writing to pool-cleaning businesses: the going price for a clean in the reader's postcode, taken across the market, with the price that won the most work.

The trap is a benchmark with no next step. Telling them they're behind gives them a bad feeling and nothing to do about it. Always pair it with what the ones ahead did.

## 2. The rework of their own thing

**Something they made, done better, so they can see the difference immediately.**

You need their material, which is usually public: their website, their quote template, their job ad, their reviews, their newsletter, their proposal, their brochure.

It fits when what you sell improves exactly that kind of thing, so the rework is a free sample of the work itself.

- A copywriter writing to a software firm: their pricing page rewritten, with the three lines changed and why each one changes.
- A dental marketing firm writing to a practice: a reply written for each of their unanswered Google reviews, in a sheet they can copy from.
- A sales trainer writing to a firm hiring a sales manager: their job ad's list of what the new hire must fix, each line answered with what it takes in the first ninety days.

The trap is doing too much. Rework one thing well, show them the before and after, and stop. A full rebuild of their website is free consultancy and reads as desperate.

## 3. The list they'd have to build themselves

**Names, sites, projects or buyers they'd want to know about, pulled together from public records.**

You need a public source with dates on it (planning portals, tenders, company filings, licence registers, event exhibitor lists, permit data) and the time to join it up.

It fits when what you sell helps them win work, and the list is the work they could be winning.

- A scaffolding firm writing to builders: every planning approval for a loft conversion within ten miles in the last month, with the address and the date.
- An equipment hire firm writing to contractors: the jobs starting nearby that need the machine the reader probably has standing idle.
- A packaging supplier writing to food brands: every supermarket listing announced in their category this quarter, with who won it.

The trap is a list that's only useful to you. If the list is really your own prospecting with their name on it, they'll see it.

## 4. The warning, with the evidence

**A risk you can see from outside, with the proof, before it costs them.**

You need a public record or something you can see with your own eyes: a certificate's expiry date, a regulation coming into force, a review trend, a photo, a lapsed registration.

It fits when you sell the fix for that risk, and the risk is real and close.

- A fire safety firm writing to a landlord: the fire alarm certificate on the unit that changed hands normally goes with the old tenant, and the inspection date for that street.
- A compliance firm writing to care agencies: the new rule coming into force on a named date, and the two things inspectors have marked agencies down on since it was announced.
- A drone survey firm writing to a site manager: a photo of an unattended hazard on their site from last week's flight, attached.

The trap is the threat. Cold, a warning can read as "I have something on you". Frame it as what others got caught by, as the value test explains, and never sound pleased about the risk.

## 5. The money they're missing

**Revenue or savings you can see in public data that they haven't taken.**

You need records that show where money goes: tenders they didn't bid on, suppliers they use, prices they pay that have dropped, grants they qualify for and haven't claimed.

It fits when what you sell closes that gap.

- A grant adviser writing to a manufacturer: the two grants open now that a firm their size in their sector qualifies for, with the closing dates.
- A procurement firm writing to a group that owns several businesses: the three things all of them buy, where the price has fallen this year, and what that gap probably adds up to across the group.
- A distributor writing to installers: the installers who fit their product but buy most of their stock from a rival, with the jobs listed.

The trap is the made-up figure. If you can't count it, say it's a rough figure, show what it rests on, and say what you don't know about their numbers.

## 6. The made-for-them sample

**A small piece of your actual work, done on their case, already finished.**

You need enough about them to make it real (their logo, their product, the problem named in their own words) and the time to make it.

It fits when your work is hard to imagine until someone sees it, and when the sample takes minutes to make once you've built the first one.

- A training firm writing to a firm whose job ad names a sales method: the opening of a short training video on that method, with their logo on it.
- A web designer writing to a care agency: a mock-up of the page a family sees first, in the agency's colours.
- A consultancy writing to a new operations director: a draft of the first ninety days for the problem their predecessor's leaving post named.

The trap is the gift they need a call to receive. The sample is attached or linked, and they can look at it without replying.

## 7. The introduction, or the person who moved

**A person they should know about, with the reason.**

You need public information about people moving or appearing: case studies that name a customer, job changes, new appointments, speakers announced.

It fits when a connection is worth something to them and you can see why.

- A software firm writing to a seller: three people who praised them in public case studies have moved to new companies, and the new companies look like good prospects.
- A recruiter writing to someone just made redundant: three firms hiring for exactly their role, with the hiring manager's name.
- An event organiser writing to a supplier: the buyer from their biggest target account is speaking next month, and the session time.

The trap is an introduction that only helps you. If the person you're pointing them to is your own prospect, it's a referral scheme.

## 8. The playbook for the moment they're in

**What the businesses who got through this exact moment well did first.**

You need expertise from having seen the moment many times, or a pattern from your own customers.

It fits when the moment is the trigger itself: a new licence, a new hire, a merger, a new site, a new season, a launch.

- An agency writing to a pharmaceutical firm whose medicine has just been licensed: the four things the launches that took off did in their first month.
- A consultancy writing to a newly appointed marketing director: what the new directors who kept the job past a year changed first, and what they left alone.
- A retail adviser writing to a brand launching a new season: what sold through fastest last season in their category, and the week it started selling.

The trap is generic advice. "Get your messaging right" is useless. The playbook names things specific enough that a reader in that moment says "I hadn't thought of that".

---

## Mixing them

The strongest gifts often combine two kinds. A list (3) with a warning (4) on each row. A rework (2) of their job ad with the playbook (8) for the hire's first ninety days. A benchmark (1) pointing at money they're missing (5). Score the combination as one gift, and keep it to one idea the reader can say back in a sentence.
