# The right moment, and finding the accounts

## The moment comes from the problem

Work backwards. Start with the expensive problem the user solves, then ask what event would make that problem urgent this month, then find the public record that carries that event with a date on it. Working forwards from whatever is easy to scrape gives you the funding round and the new job title, which everyone else is using too.

Triggers that work for nearly everyone:

- job changes, and a new senior hire
- mergers and acquisitions
- licences and permits granted
- a job ad for the role that owns the problem the user solves
- a new site, a new office, a new product launch or a new season
- a contract won, or a contract lost
- a regulation or inspection date landing
- a planning approval
- funding, only when the problem is one that money creates (hiring fast, scaling a team)

How the moment looked for a few businesses in the Sync Selling room: a pharmaceutical agency writes when a new medicine gets its licence; a marketing agency writes when a firm makes a new senior marketing hire; a product business writes at a new launch or a new season; a sales trainer writes when a firm advertises for a sales enablement or sales manager role.

## Four tests every trigger passes

A trigger that fails one is dropped, not softened.

1. **Public and checkable, with a date.** It's on a record the reader could look up. If it came from a guess or a tool that can't show its working, the email can't state it as fact.
2. **Fresh enough to still be true.** Inside its own window. A job ad that's closed, a licence from two years ago or a hire who's been in post a year are all stale.
3. **It implies the problem.** Take the trigger out of the email. If the email still makes complete sense, the trigger was decoration.
4. **It reaches a person who can act.** The event happens to a company, and the email goes to a person. Name the role who both feels the problem and can say yes.

## What it proves, and what it only suggests

Separate the two, out loud, for every trigger. A planning approval proves a build is coming. It only suggests they haven't chosen a contractor. The email states the first as fact and softens the second ("that often means", "normally when"), because they might have sorted it already. Reversing the two is how you get the reply that says you don't know their business.

## The sample comes before the list

Before anything is researched in depth, find a sample of twenty businesses at the moment, and show them to the user.

1. Search for businesses at the moment, using web search, the public records named in the brief, job boards, registers and news. Record the source and the date for each.
2. Take twenty. Show the user a table: company, the trigger, the date, the source link, why it fits.
3. The user marks each one right or wrong. Read the wrong ones carefully, because almost every trigger catches one kind of business it shouldn't (recruitment agencies posting for clients, subsidiaries of giant groups, consumer businesses that look like trade ones). Write the false positives into the brief so the next search leaves them out.
4. Count. From the sample, how many businesses hit this moment each week or month? That number decides how many emails will go out, and the number of emails decides the channel (see `sending.md`).

If the count is tiny, the trigger is too narrow, or the segment is. If it's in the thousands, it's a market rather than a segment, and the emails will drift generic. An account-based campaign works best on tens to a few hundred businesses a month.

## Researching one account

The research agent (`agents/1-research.md`) builds one pack per account. The rules it follows:

- **Public sources only, each with a link and the date it was read.** Their website, their job ads, company filings, their reviews, news, the people's public profiles, public registers.
- **Their own words, quoted exactly.** What they say about their business, what their ad asks for, what their customers say in reviews. The writer uses their words for things: if they call their salespeople account managers, so does the email.
- **The fact table.** Every fact the email might use goes in a table with its source. A sentence in the email that can't point to a row comes out.
- **Nothing personal that isn't professional.** Their role, their time in post, what they've said publicly about their work. Never their family, their holidays or their politics.
- **Enough, then stop.** The pack serves the gift and the email. Twenty facts nobody will use make the writer's job harder.

## Finding the person and their address

- Find the person who owns the problem: the role named in the brief. If the trigger is a job ad, the hiring manager is usually the person the new hire reports to.
- Find a second senior person at the same business if there is one, for the cadence's later emails.
- Find their email with whatever tool the user has. Where two sources give the same address, mark it confident. Where only one does, mark it for the user to decide. Never guess a pattern (first.last@) and send to it unchecked, because bounces damage the sending address.
- Record where each address came from. Many countries' privacy laws expect you to be able to say.

## Two things never to do

- **Stack triggers in the opener.** "Congratulations on the new office, the funding and the new hire" reads as a file someone's been keeping on them. One moment, the one that implies the problem.
- **Build the full list before writing the first email.** Write the email for three accounts from the sample first. If the opening line is weak with a real example in it, the trigger is weak, and no amount of research fixes it.
