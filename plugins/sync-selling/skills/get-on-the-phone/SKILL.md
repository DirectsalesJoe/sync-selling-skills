# Get on the phone

Most deals are won or lost on whether you get a conversation, because a conversation is the one place you learn what the buyer cares about, and the only place you can show them you're different. An email gets compared on price. A call gets you heard.

Joe's view, from the sessions: "It's less about the channel and more about how specific the message is." If the reason to talk is genuinely useful to them, it hardly matters how it's worded. If it isn't, no wording saves it.

Always try to call first, and get them on the phone by offering something of benefit to them that leaves them curious. Write only to set the call up.

## The four tests

Every message this skill writes passes all four before it goes. Read `references/moments.md` for the lines that pass them.

1. **About them.** The reason to talk is something only this buyer would get: their job, their tender, their drawings, what they told you. "I'd love to tell you about us" fails. "A couple of things came up when I was going through your enquiry" passes.
2. **Something in it for them.** A fix, a finding, a risk spotted, or the chance to be heard. Give before you ask. "I've written some thoughts down" builds reciprocity, "a couple of things" builds curiosity without satisfying it, and "might save you going back to market" names what they'd lose by not talking.
3. **Small and time-boxed.** Three minutes on the phone, ten minutes on a call. Never "a meeting", never open-ended.
4. **Easy to say no.** "Would you be dead against a ten-minute conversation?" asks for a no, and people find it easier to say yes to a question that lets them off. To someone who's already pushed back, the softener goes first: "Feel free to tell me to get back in my box."

And one rule underneath: one ask per message, and never "just checking in" or "touching base". Those say you have nothing to give.

## How to run it

1. **Find the moment.** Ask the user who it's for, what's happened so far and what they want from the call. Match it to a moment in `references/moments.md`. If two fit, take the earlier one in the buyer's journey.
2. **Find the reason.** Ask what they've noticed: something in the drawings, the enquiry, the tender, the buyer's plans, or something the buyer said last time. If they attach documents, read them and suggest two or three reasons, each tied to something on the page. If there's no reason worth the buyer's time, say so and stop, because a message with nothing in it does more harm than no message.
3. **Write the set.**
   - The message that sets up the call (phone first; an email, text or LinkedIn note only to get the call).
   - The first thirty seconds when they pick up: who you are, the reason, the small ask.
   - The questions for the call, from the moment's list, five at most.
   - The line that books the next step before they hang up.
4. **Check it.** Run the four tests on every message and show the user which line passes each. Flag anything you've assumed about the buyer that the user didn't tell you, and any figure without a source. Never invent a figure, a job or a quote.

## What to give back

The moment you chose and why, in one line. The reason to talk. Then the message, the opening, the questions and the booking line, ready to copy, with anything in [square brackets] for the user to fill. Finish with the four tests, one line each.

## Before you hang up, every time

The easiest way to get someone back on the phone is never to let them go without the next call in the diary. "On the back of this, I'll put together [what you're making], and walk you through it so you can change anything that doesn't hit the mark. How's [day] at [time]?" Book it while you're both still on the line, and you'll never have to chase.
